Latest Colvin Grants Assist Youth-Focused DXpeditions
-----------------------------------------------------
Two upcoming DXpeditions with a focus on young operators are the latest
recipients of Colvin grants from ARRL. The grants, funded by an endowment
established by noted DXpeditioner Lloyd Colvin, W6KG (SK), are intended to
support amateur radio projects that promote international goodwill in the
field of DX.

The latest recipients are the upcoming VK9XY DXpedition to Christmas Island,
south of Indonesia - scheduled for this November 16 through December 4 - and
the VPSG expedition to South Georgia Island, in the South Atlantic, planned
for March 2027.

Ten of the 12 VK9XY operators are under age 26, and the team leader is ARRL
Education and Learning Support Specialist Max Freedman, N4ML, already a
veteran DXpeditioner at age 22. But the truly unique aspect of this
expedition are plans for up to 40 young hams to operate the station remotely
from various parts of the world, using an internet-linked "rig in a box," or
RIB.

"The VK9XY team would like to sincerely thank ARRL granting us the Colvin
Award," said Freedman. "It is only with this collective support from groups
like NCDXF, DX Engineering, ARRL, and the community at large that we are
able to bring the next generation of radio amateurs right to the bleeding
edge of DXpeditioning."

The VPSG team will also include three (on-site) young operators and will be
the first amateur radio activation of South Georgia since 2016. (One young
operator, Violetta Latham, KN2P, is part of both the VK9XY and VPSG teams!)
In addition to the challenges associated with getting to and operating from
a sub-Antarctic island, the team also has to negotiate very strict
environmental regulations. According to the team's website, the group is
committed to "operating with the highest possible standards of environmental
responsibility" while maximizing the number of unique contacts to give
smaller stations a better opportunity to work South Georgia.


San Diego Balloon Launch Draws in High School Students
------------------------------------------------------
Members of the Mt. Carmel High School Amateur Radio Club in San Diego,
California, used a high-altitude balloon launch October 17 to generate
interest among their fellow students and the community in amateur radio,
science, technology, engineering and math. Members of the public were
encouraged to track the balloon flight in real time using an app called
Sondehub-Amateur, which displays telemetry from amateur radio high altitude
balloon launches.

This flight, which carried the call sign K6GO, belonging to Gayle Olson, a
volunteer for the ARRL Foundation Scholarship Committee and Maxim Society
member, flew to an altitude of 17-1/2 miles before the balloon burst and the
payload parachuted back to the ground in Julian, California, some 60 miles
inland from its launch point on Fiesta Island. The launch was part of the
San Diego Repeater Association's annual "Hamtastic" event, which brings
together hams and clubs from throughout the region to get on the air,
socialize, and demonstrate amateur radio to the public. The San Diego
Repeater Association is an ARRL Affiliated Club.

Hams Help "Run" the Chicago Marathon
------------------------------------
The 2026 Chicago Marathon on October 11 drew 55,000 runners from around the
world and 165 ham radio operators from 6 states to help provide medical
communications and logistical support. This was the 18th year of amateur
radio participation, according to Rob Orr, K9RST, president of Ham Radio
Chicago, which coordinated amateur radio activity at the marathon. The group
also helps recruit new volunteers and provides training in public service
communications.

Orr said the hams relied on 5 repeaters with citywide coverage as well as 5
portable UHF tactical repeaters to make sure every amateur radio team had at
least three ways to contact the command tent in Grant Park. In addition to
traditional FM voice communications, Orr said the hams also use DMR (digital
mobile radio) and, this year, introduced a Zoom channel on which medical
personnel could have private consults if needed. The hams coordinated the
calls on the radio and then got the parties together at a Zoom station in
the command tent.

One hundred radio amateurs were stationed at medical tents along the course,
as well as a medical hub, passing along the medical teams' requests for
transport, supplies, and other information. The hams also provide
situational awareness to the event organizers, reporting on the number of
patients being cared for at each medical tent, as well as the stress levels
the medical teams face in providing care. Orr notes that amateur radio is
just one component of a very complex event that requires 15,000 volunteers
to succeed. However, the hams' role is unique, he points out, adding that
"this event has shown the public, city officials, and the runners that ham
radio is very much alive and doing well."

A Century-Plus of Radio Innovation: The 102nd ARRL Handbook is Now Available
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
First published in 1926, The ARRL Handbook has helped generations of radio
amateurs turn curiosity into knowledge, ideas into circuits, and experiments
into new discoveries. Over the past century, it has evolved alongside radio
technology, reflecting new developments and inspiring hands-on learning and
experimentation.

Edited by Gregory D. Lapin, Ph.D., P.E., N9GL, the 102nd edition continues
The ARRL Handbook's long history of technical excellence, with updated
coverage of radio electronics, circuit design, antennas, propagation,
digital communications, testing, and troubleshooting. New and expanded
topics include signals below the noise, AI and FreeDV, digital mesh
networks, satellite communications, compact and stealth antennas, NanoVNA
applications, software defined radio (SDR), and microwave technology.

Now shipping, the 102nd edition is available in a six-volume set, a
softcover edition, and a digital edition. The print editions include the
digital edition and more than 350 supplemental files, including articles,
projects, software, PC board templates, and support files.

From understanding fundamental radio principles to experimenting with
emerging technologies, The ARRL Handbook continues to give radio amateurs
the knowledge and inspiration to build, experiment, and innovate.

Historic LF station SAQ will be on the air on October 23 and 24 to mark
United Nations Day on the 24th. The Alexanderson alternator-based Grimeton
Radio Station in Sweden transmits on 17.2 kHz, and operated as a commercial
radio station from 1924 until the early 1990s. It is now a World Heritage

24th at 1245 UTC. The event will be livestreamed on YouTube. In addition,
amateur station SK6SAQ will be on the air on the 24th, operating CW on 80,
40, and 20 meters, as well as SSB on 75 and 40 meters. Reception reports for
SAQ may be submitted online for an E-QSL card. QSLs for SK6SAQ contacts may
be sent via the SM bureau, by email to info@xxxxxxxxx.x.xxx or by postal
mail to Alexander Association, Radiostationen 72, SE-432 98 Grimeton, Sweden.

In Brief...
-----------
A helicopter pilot was killed after his aircraft crashed into a radio tower
believed to belong to a well-known ham in Florida. According to various news
reports, the pilot worked for the Manatee County Mosquito Control District
and had been conducting aerial spraying on October 15 in an effort to
control the spread of a mosquito-borne illness. He was reportedly returning
to his base when he struck what was described as a 150-foot private radio
tower in Myakka City, east of Sarasota. The tower apparently belonged to
well-known contester Dan Street, K1TO, and was the tallest of three on his
property. ARRL has reached out to Street for additional information.

The YASME Foundation has presented its 2026 YASME Excellence Award to the
team behind the Reverse Beacon Network, or RBN, in recognition of their
"support of a global volunteer-operated network of automated amateur radio
receiver stations (SDR skimmers) that listen to radio bands and report who
is transmitting in near-real time." According to the foundation, the RBN
typically has 250 active nodes around the world measuring a station's
frequency, signal strength, signal-to-noise ratio, and sending speed, all of
which is then reported live to a central RBN database. Amateurs use this
data for many purposes, including propagation testing, antenna comparisons,
contest operating and results adjudication. The RBN team consists of founder
Felipe Ceglia, PY1NB; Nick Sinanis, F5VIH; Mark Glenn, K7MJG; Dave Pascoe,
KM3T; Pete Smith, N4ZR; Dick Williams, W3OA, and Chris White, WX7V.

October 31 is the deadline for schools to apply for grants from ARRL's
Education and Technology Program (ETP). The grants assist schools and
teachers in the purchase of radio equipment, software, and other
radio-related resources for school ham stations. The program offers two
types of grants: ETP School Station Grants for schools providing a plan to
use amateur radio as part of in-classroom learning or an enrichment program;
and ETP Progress Grants for teachers already using amateur radio in their
classrooms to purchase additional resources or for those teachers needing
resources in order to start teaching about electronics and wireless
technology using amateur radio. Details are on the ETP page of the ARRL
website.

Former ARRL Membership Services Manager Charles "Chuck" Hutchinson, K8CH, of
Lake Odessa, Michigan, became a Silent Key on October 10, 2026 at age 86.
Chuck was born on August 25, 1940, in Lansing, Michigan. He earned a
bachelor's degree in TV/radio broadcasting from Michigan State University,
while working at the university as a TV engineer. He later worked for IBM
servicing computers and spent 3 years in Ecuador as an engineer for
shortwave station HCJB before coming to work at ARRL Headquarters in
Newington, Connecticut. Chuck retired after 20 years at ARRL and moved back
to Michigan, but continued to contribute to amateur radio through various
ARRL publications, including Simple and Fun Antennas for Hams. He is
survived by his wife, Sylvia, two sons, and a granddaughter. A complete
obituary is online.

Students and faculty members at the University of Scranton in Pennsylvania
have been working for the past year and a half with five clients of the
Lackawanna Blind Association to teach them ham radio and help them get their
Technician licenses. All five have now successfully earned their licenses,
and are regulars on the university amateur radio club's W3USR repeater,
according to club advisor Dr. Nathaniel Frissell, W2NAF. Frissell says the
club used training materials from the Handiham program, and that at a recent
graduation ceremony, each of the new licensees was presented with a tactile
plaque containing their name and call sign, 3D-printed and in Braille. The
program was recently highlighted on the university's online news site, Royal
News. The University of Scranton Amateur Radio Club is an ARRL Affiliated
Club.

HAARP, the University of Alaska Fairbanks High Frequency Active Auroral
Research Program, is in the middle of a research campaign that will result
in high-powered transmissions on frequencies between 2.8 and 10 MHz (but not
within the 80- or 40-meter amateur bands). The research campaign began on
October 20 and will continue through October 24, with transmissions on
varying frequencies between 0530 and 0930 UTC daily. According to the
university, these transmissions are being conducted in support of the
INFUSE-II and DUST-III sounding rocket missions. INFUSE-II will test a new
type of ultraviolet imager for use on future space telescopes, while
DUST-III will carry a miniature laboratory into space to study how tiny
grains of space dust form and grow.

The ARRL Solar Report
---------------------
The active sunspot region that has been giving us improved propagation over
the past several days will be rotating away from Earth's view today, but
conditions on most of the HF amateur bands should remain pretty decent in
the days ahead, as the 10.7-centimeter radio flux remains right around 100.
However, NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) says the geomagnetic
field could become unsettled to active heading into the weekend as a
negative polarity coronal hole high-speed stream (CHHSS) heads toward Earth.

The Australian Space Weather Forecasting Centre predicts mostly normal HF
propagation conditions worldwide.

The W3LPL Propagation Forecast from the Daily DX is predicting a dropoff in
DX openings on 10 and 12 meters as the active sunspot region rotates away
from Earth and the solar cycle continues its decline. However, good
conditions are expected to continue on 15, 17, and 20 meters, with the lower
HF bands (30, 40 and 80 meters) providing excellent long distance nighttime
propagation.

This weekend is the CQ World Wide SSB DX Contest (0000Z October 24 through
2359 Z October 25). It's often said that this contest makes its own
propagation, most likely because there are stations active to take advantage
of unanticipated openings, even on the higher HF bands. So keep listening
for calls from faraway stations.

Predicted 10.7-centimeter solar flux: Oct 22-23, 100; Oct 24-26, 95; Oct 27,
100; Oct 28-29, 105. (Source: NOAA SWPC)
