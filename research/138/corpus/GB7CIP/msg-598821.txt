AMSAT Youth Initiative Featured in Florida News
The following article was published in the Main Street Daily News
of Gainesville, Florida on October 12, 2026 under the headline,
"2 Alachua County schools develop student-built technology to
communicate with satellites":
Two Alachua County schools are developing hands-on science,
technology, engineering and mathematics (STEM) programs that
could give students the opportunity to communicate with
satellites orbiting Earth and, potentially, astronauts aboard the
International Space Station (ISS).
At the 44th annual conference of the Radio Amateur Satellite
Corporation (AMSAT), held in Jacksonville, local educator Gordon
Gibby presented the schoolsâ efforts on Oct. 9, according to a
North Florida Amateur Radio Club (NFARC) press release. Students
at Gainesville Christian Community School (GCCS) and The Rock
School are learning the technical skills needed to build
satellite-tracking systems and establish radio communications
with spacecraft.

Frank Karnauskas, N1UW, AMSAT Vice President &#8211; Development,
who heads up the youth educational initiatives, giving a talk
about their free available materials for youth. (Photo courtesy
of the North Florida Amateur Radio Club.)
AMSAT is a nonprofit, volunteer organization dedicated to
advancing amateur radio satellite technology and education. Its
resources and activities help make satellite communications a
practical learning opportunity for students.
Gainesville Christian Community School Expands Technology
Curriculum
At GCCS, mathematics teacher John Seppala is teaching a class of
16 students, primarily at the middle-school level, using a
textbook developed by the school that covers a broad range of
practical technologies.
The curriculum explores systems and skills used to maintain a
home and vehicle, including electricity, electronics, 3D
printing, small engines, plumbing, amateur radio and computers.
Seppala is preparing to take the multiple-choice examination
required to obtain his Federal Communications Commission (FCC)
amateur radio license. Many of his students are expected to
pursue licenses as well, potentially allowing them to participate
in radio communications experiments and satellite operations.
The Rock School Students Build Satellite-Tracking Station
At The Rock School, 10 high school students have formed an
Amateur Radio Satellite Club. Organized into six overlapping
teams, the students are developing a robotic antenna-control
system and radio station designed to track and communicate with
low-Earth-orbit satellites and the ISS.
The project combines electronics, mechanical engineering,
computer programming and fabrication. Students are assembling a
robotic control board, learning to solder electronic components
and preparing to use a 3D printer to manufacture gears for the
antennaâs elevation and rotation mechanism.
Future steps include building the antenna support system with
aluminum masts, U-bolts and high-gain antennas. Students are also
studying independently for the FCC examinations required to
obtain amateur radio licenses.
âThis is exciting!â said Lacy Basford, the schoolâs academic
adviser, in the release.
Rather than purchasing a commercial antenna-rotator controller,
students plan to build their own system using an Arduino Nano
microcomputer and a custom-designed printed circuit board. They
will adapt open-source software written by a U.S. amateur radio
operator, manufacture mechanical components and assemble the
system themselves.
The project will bring together mathematics, electronics,
software and mechanical design, allowing students to see how
those disciplines work together in a functioning system.
Potential Opportunity to Communicate With Astronauts
Gibby hopes both schools will eventually be ready to apply to
host a scheduled radio contact through Amateur Radio on the
International Space Station (ARISS), an international educational
initiative that connects students with astronauts through amateur
radio.
If selected, students could have the opportunity to ask questions
of crew members aboard the ISS through a radio link. The process
requires an educational proposal, technical preparation and
formal selection, and both schools are in the early stages of
developing their programs.
Preparing for a potential contact involves more than learning to
operate a radio. Students must understand orbital motion, radio-
wave propagation, antenna direction, electronics and the
challenges of maintaining a reliable communications link with a
fast-moving spacecraft.
Students Learn to Track Satellites and Account for Doppler Shift
At the Jacksonville conference, participants demonstrated a life-
sized, operational simulator of small amateur radio satellites.
The simulator replicates aspects of a satellite radio system,
including the Doppler shift that changes a signalâs apparent
frequency as a satellite approaches and moves away from a ground
station.
The equipment can simulate several types of amateur radio
satellites, including those that handle digital data, repeat
voice transmissions and relay Morse code.
The simulator could help students learn to recognize and
compensate for Doppler shifts while tracking satellites overhead,
maintaining communications as their apparent signal frequencies
change. The experience connects classroom physics to a practical
engineering challenge in satellite communications.
Looking Beyond the International Space Station
The conference also featured a presentation by Frank Bauer, an
engineer and geologist who serves as ARISS executive director.
Bauer discussed the organizationâs work supporting educational
radio contacts with the ISS and its engagement with the future of
human spaceflight.
Communications with future space stations and, eventually, lunar
facilities offer a longer-term source of inspiration for students
learning radio technology. AMSAT Vice President Frank Karnauskas
also discussed educational materials developed by AMSAT for young
people.
For students in Alachua County, the immediate goals are to build
a functioning satellite-tracking station, earn amateur radio
licenses and develop the technical and educational foundation
needed to pursue an ARISS contact.
Resources Available for Other Schools
According to the release, Gibby said the projects could serve as
models for other schools interested in incorporating practical
engineering and space communications into their STEM programs.
With volunteer support, open-source software, affordable
microcomputers and student-built components, schools can offer
meaningful technical experiences without relying entirely on
expensive commercial systems.
Resources for schools interested in amateur radio satellite
communications and ARISS educational opportunities are available
through the local Amateur Radio Emergency Service website at
https://www.nf4rc.club/ariss-satellite-resources/.

successfully launched on October 1 on a SpaceX Falcon 9 rideshare
from Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. Developed by

stored SSTV images and digipeater payloads. Two of the three
satellites have now been officially designated as OSCARs &#8212;
Orbiting Satellites Carrying Amateur Radio!

problem.

Where Does Space Begin?
There is no line where the atmosphere stops. It thins the way a
beach gets wetter towards the sea, until at some point you are in
the water and nobody can say exactly when that happened. So the
edge of space is not something anyone discovered. It is something
people chose, and they chose differently.
For decades that disagreement cost almost nobody anything. It is
starting to. Tourist rockets fly into the disputed band with
paying passengers and come back down. Hypersonic military
vehicles pass through it. The question of which number is right
has turned into a question about astronaut wings, ticket sales
and, eventually, sovereign airspace.
Three numbers
The familiar figure is 100 kilometres, the KÃ¡rmÃ¡n line. The FAI,
the world air sports records body, uses it to separate aviation
records from astronautics records. A 2023 FAI presentation by
Nicolas BÃ©rend says the federation set it in 1960, with a group

aeronautics and astronautics recordsâ. The name came slightly
earlier: Andrew G. Haley coined âKÃ¡rmÃ¡n lineâ in a 1957 paper
after discussions with KÃ¡rmÃ¡n. That is what the 100 kilometre
figure is, a records-keeping convention with a date on it.
The American figure is 50 miles, about 80 kilometres. The US Air
Force used it for its astronaut wings, and the Federal Aviation
Administration used the same threshold for the commercial
astronaut wings it handed out until it ended that programme after
2021. In 2005 NASA gave civilian astronaut wings to three X-15
pilots, retired NASA pilot Bill Dana and the families of the late
John B. McKay and Joseph A. Walker, acknowledging âtheir flights
above 264,000 feet altitudeâ. That is 50 miles. When those
flights happened only the military had wings to hand out, so
civilian pilots flew just as high and got no badge. Danaâs first
flight into space took him 58.13 miles above the Mojave Desert on
1 November 1966.
The legal figure does not exist. The 1944 Chicago Convention
gives every state complete and exclusive sovereignty over the
airspace above its territory. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty says
outer space is not subject to national appropriation. Neither
treaty says where one ends and the other begins, a gap a 2017
briefing to the UN space committeeâs legal subcommittee lays out
plainly. The committee has had the item on its agenda since 1966.
Inside it the USSR repeatedly proposed 100 or 110 kilometres and
the United States rejected any definition at all. The
subcommitteeâs 2026 draft report still carries âMatters relating
to the definition and delimitation of outer spaceâ, sixty years

What the physics says
The scientific answer depends on what question is actually being
asked. KÃ¡rmÃ¡n himself was blunt about this. In 1961 lectures
quoted in the FAI presentation he said that where atmosphere ends
and space begins âis a judiciary rather than a scientific
problem,â and he proposed a range, 300,000 to 400,000 feet,
roughly 91 to 122 kilometres, rather than a line. The 100
kilometre figure was a round number inside that range.
Jonathan McDowell, an astronomer who has worked at the Harvard-
Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, made the satellite case in a
2018 paper in Acta Astronautica, âThe edge of space: Revisiting
the Karman Lineâ, free to read on arXiv. He looked at the orbits
of real satellites. An elliptical orbit whose lowest point sits
at 100 kilometres can survive for long periods. A satellite whose
lowest point drops below 80 kilometres is âhighly unlikely to
complete its next orbitâ. That lowest point is the perigee.
McDowell argues that this makes roughly 80 kilometres a useful
boundary.
Two other things happen to land in the same place. Meteors,
moving much faster than satellites, usually break apart somewhere
in the 70 to 100 kilometre range, a sign that the atmosphere
starts to bite there. The mesopause, a boundary layer in the
upper atmosphere, lies in the same broad region, though its
height varies with latitude and season and is not fixed at 80
kilometres. McDowell also notes that 80 kilometres falls within
500 metres of the old 50-mile astronaut wings line. Those are
separate ideas that land in roughly the same place. He suggests
adopting exactly 80 kilometres as a better convention, one that
fits what satellites actually do.
Why the 20 kilometres now matter
On 11 July 2021 Richard Branson flew on Virgin Galacticâs Unity
22 to an apogee of about 86 kilometres. Nine days later Jeff
Bezos flew on Blue Originâs New Shepard past 100. Two days before
Bransonâs flight, Blue Origin posted that New Shepard was
designed to fly above the KÃ¡rmÃ¡n line âso none of our astronauts
have an asterisk next to their nameâ. By McDowellâs number, and
by the US number, Bransonâs passengers went to space. By the
FAIâs, they did not. Virgin Galactic has kept selling tickets in
that gap ever since, even through its current pause in flights
while it builds its next ships. The dispute is no longer about an
X-15 pilotâs badge. It is about what a company can truthfully
print on a brochure.
The military version is less visible and more serious. For a
vehicle passing over another country at 90 kilometres, an agreed
boundary could help determine whether it had entered sovereign
airspace. Neither an astronaut-wings rule nor an air sports
convention settles that question. Nor would being in space put a
weapon beyond the law: the Outer Space Treaty says activities
there are carried out in accordance with international law,
including the UN Charter. An agreed boundary could clarify the
sovereignty question, although it would not by itself settle the
legality of a weaponâs passage or use. It is the piece that is

Falcon 9 Launches Bandwagon-5 Rideshare Mission
Following on from the successful Transporter 18 mission at the
beginning of the month, SpaceXâs Falcon 9 vehicle launched an
additional 32 rideshare payloads for the Bandwagon-5 mission on
Tuesday, Oct. 20, delayed by a day due to unfavorable recovery
weather.Â Falcon 9 lifted off from Space Launch Complex 40
(SLC-40) at the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. A
one-hour launch window opens at 10:30 UTC, with the vehicle
following a southeasterly trajectory to low-Earth orbit.
SpaceXâs Transporter and Bandwagon rideshare missions allow small
satellite companies and government and educational institutions
to launch payloads of up to 50 kg for $350,000, significantly
cheaper than a full Falcon 9 launch. This offers a more
affordable and straightforward route to space for many companies.
Alongside the rideshare payloads, SpaceX is launching the second
of its Starfall capsules, which is mounted atop the satellite
stack.
SpaceX included its second Starfall capsule on the Bandwagon 5
mission. The disk-shaped reentry vehicle can remain in space
indefinitely and can reenter and be recovered by SpaceX at any
time. The capsule measures 3.1 m in diameter and 0.75 m in
height, has a mass of 2,100 kg, and can carry up to 1,000 kg of
payload. SpaceX will use the vehicle for point-to-point cargo
delivery and for transporting goods manufactured in microgravity.
SpaceX will ultimately launch the capsules aboard its super-
heavy-lift Starship, with multiple capsules likely to launch
aboard the vehicle at one time.
Exolaunch, a launch services and mission management provider, is
manifesting 22 customer satellites on Bandwagon-5. Other payloads
include multiple synthetic aperture radar (SAR) Earth observation
satellites from ICEYE, two high-resolution SAR satellites from
Capella Space, and two commercial SAR payloads from Umbra Space.
SAR satellites use remote sensing to produce high-resolution
imagery in any weather conditions, at any time of day. In
addition to supporting military applications, SAR can also
measure and observe Earthâs surface features in extreme detail.

OsTrax Now Available for Windows, macOS, and Ubuntu
Avinstar, LLC has announced the general availability of OsTrax,
an orbiting satellite tracking and operating platform for
Windows, macOS and Ubuntu.
Satellite communication presents one of the more interesting and
involved opportunities using radio. The need to know when and
where a satellite is at a given time, while carefully
coordinating radios and antennas to communicate using the
satellite presents many challenges.
OsTrax addresses these challenges by incorporating a fresh,
modern approach combined with a central satellite information
repository to eliminate problems associated with obtaining
accurate and timely satellite information. The result is more
operating time with less effort.
OsTrax is user customizable to create a personalized operating
experience with full control of satellite selection and satellite
pass control.
OsTrax controls radios and antenna rotors based on the
requirements of a satellite pass and communication type. Radios
and rotor selections extend from legacy to current.
Automatic updating of orbital elements and satellite profile
information ensure the latest operating information is always
available.
User defined orbital elements and satellite profiles provide
access opportunities for new or exploratory satellites and
features.
OsTrax enhances operating with simplified contact logging and
advanced tools to determine future satellite pass predictions and
perform satellite pass modeling.
In addition to satellite tracking, OsTrax tracks the Sun and Moon
with antenna rotor control.

Changes to AMSAT TLE Distribution for Oct. 23, 2026
Two Line Elements or TLEs, often referred to as Keplerian
elements or keps in the amateur community, are the inputs to the
SGP4 standard mathematical model of spacecraft orbits used by
most amateur tracking programs. Weekly updates are completely
adequate for most amateur satellites. TLE bulletin files are
updated daily in the first hour of the UTC day. New bulletin
files will be posted immediately after reliable elements become
available for new amateur satellites. More information may be
found at https://www.amsat.org/keplerian-elements-resources/.

General Perturbations Data Support
AMSAT is pleased to announce that modern forms of what are called
General Perturbations data are being disseminated via modern
formats including JSON, XML and KVN at
https://newark192.amsat.org/gpdata/current/. The reason this
change is being made is that we are running out of 5-digit
catalog numbers and the TLE format is not viable for satellites
launched after July of this year. See
https://celestrak.org/NORAD/documentation/gp-data-formats.php for
details.
These data are presently considered in beta test for the next two
months while hosted on the test server newark192.amsat.org, and
we are very open to community feedback at webmaster at amsat.org.
Testers may experience outages and errors while we make
improvements. We intend to put this into production on our main
web server in July as we expect that satellites launched after
this summer will require one of the new formats to accommodate
longer object numbers. AMSAT will continue to publish TLE
bulletins for satellites launched before July 2026 indefinitely.

+Â A Russian satellite just took a very close look at an American
spacecraft in low Earth orbit. The snooping satellite, Kosmos
2612, rendezvoused with ICEYE-X36, a commercial craft that

The rendezvous has garnered a lot of attention in space circles,
because it was non-consensual and startlingly close; at one

meters. There are about 80 ICEYE spacecraft in LEO at the moment.

+ NASA finally released its Request for Proposals (RFP) for the
next phase of its Commercial LEO Destinations (CLD) program on
October 9, about ten days later than promised. The CLD is the
program to replace the International Space Station (ISS) with a
privately developed orbiting platform. Seeking proposals from
industry by December 8, the agency said it would select at least
two companies by next April for initial awards to continue work
on their stations, with at least one later selected for full
development. Companies developing commercial space stations have
an array of challenges to overcome, from the ongoing NASA
competition to securing outside investment and non-NASA customers
to the technical issues of building a commercial space station.
(ANS thanks The Space Review for the above information. Read the
full story at https://www.thespacereview.com/article/5340/1.)
+ The space around Earth is turning into a junkyard. Researchers
think a refundable deposit scheme could force satellite operators
to clean up their act before a tipping point is reached. In a
paper published last month in the journal Nature Communications,
a group of scientists propose a scheme that would require
satellite operators to post a bond of $200,000 before launch,
refundable with interest upon the completion of a successful
deorbit. Companies that fail to remove their junk would lose the
money, which could go toward a shared pool funding active debris-
removal missions. (ANS thanks Space.com for the above
information. Read the full story at https://www.space.com/space-
exploration/satellites/could-satellite-deorbit-deposits-prevent-
a-space-debris-disaster.)
