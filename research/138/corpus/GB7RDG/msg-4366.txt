ARES© Briefs, Links
-------------------
An ARES©/RACES team in Texas was called out to help with communications
following an apparent carbon monoxide incident at a local middle school on
August 27. Team member Liz Brown, K5EMB, reports the school's roughly 1,200
students were evacuated to a nearby stadium. Staff members found it
difficult to communicate from one side of the facility to the other, so the
city's Emergency Management Coordinator requested help from the
Carrollton-Farmers Branch-Addison ARES©/RACES group. While the
communications problems were quickly resolved, the hams were asked to remain
and "shadow" certain school officials until all students and parents were
reunited about 4 hours later. -Thanks to Rich Moseson, W2VU, The ARRL Letter

Florida Hams Provide Communications Support During Hurricane Isaias

Amateur radio operators from Florida's Santa Rosa, Nassau, Lake, Pinellas,
and Leon Counties played an important role in supporting emergency
communications efforts during and following Hurricane Isaias, helping ensure
that critical communication capabilities remained available throughout the
storm.

In Santa Rosa County, volunteer amateur radio operators staffed the
Emergency Operations Center (EOC) and the evacuation shelter at the Milton
Community Center. These volunteers provided a communications link between
the shelter and the EOC, relaying situational updates and maintaining
readiness to support emergency communications should traditional systems
become unavailable.

Amateur radio operators across the state also supported auxiliary
communications efforts, providing additional capabilities to maintain
contact with the State Emergency Operations Center in Tallahassee if
conventional communication networks experienced disruptions.

"While traditional communications systems have become significantly more
reliable over the years, amateur radio continues to serve as a dependable
auxiliary communications resource," said Santa Rosa County Community
Emergency Response Team (CERT) and Amateur Radio Emergency Service© (ARES©)
Program Manager Arc Thames, W4CPD. "When disaster strikes, our volunteers
are trained, prepared, and ready to step in wherever we're needed. It's
another layer of resiliency that helps protect our communities."

Thames also serves as the ARRL Northern Florida Section Emergency
Coordinator and the Amateur Radio Liaison to the Florida Division of
Emergency Management, supporting the coordination of amateur radio and
auxiliary communications resources statewide.

"During tropical weather events impacting Florida, our Auxiliary
Communications team provides volunteer operators to monitor amateur radio
communications at the State Emergency Operations Center in Tallahassee,"
Thames explained. "This ensures that if someone is unable to reach emergency
officials through traditional means, we have another way to receive and
relay critical information. We always hope we're not needed, but we're glad
to be there just in case when all else fails." - Thanks to ARRL Northern
Florida SEC Arc Thames, W4CPD, and The ARRL Letter

Mississippi Active for Isaias

Between October 6 and October 9, 2026, Mississippi ARES monitored,
mobilized, and staged statewide operational readiness for Hurricane Isaias.
Initial numerical modeling and dynamic guidance presented significant spread
across the central and northern Gulf Coast, indicating potential Category 2
to Category 3 landfall between southeastern Louisiana, coastal Mississippi,
southern Alabama, and the Florida Panhandle.

Mississippi was fortunate to miss a direct strike from the hurricane. The
system rapidly intensified over unseasonably warm Gulf waters (84 øF-86 øF),
reaching peak sustained winds of 120 mph (958 mb central pressure).
Crucially, the ultimate steering pattern and center track did not solidify
until just hours prior to landfall, steering the core sharply northeastward
toward southern Alabama and the Florida Panhandle.

Because of this late eastward deflection, coastal and southeastern
Mississippi remained on the weaker western (clean) side of the circulation,
averting catastrophic storm surge (3-7 feet forecast) and widespread
hurricane-force wind damage. Mississippi ARES activated on SSB and DMR at
1200 CDST on Friday, October 9, and stood down at 2200 CDST that evening.
-ARRL Mississippi Section Emergency Coordinator Robert Hayes, KC5IMN


Amateur Radio Delivers Over 670 USGS ShakeOut "DYFI" Reports in Major
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Emergency Communications Exercise
---------------------------------
When the ground moves, seconds count-and when standard telecommunications
collapse, emergency management depends on ground-truth reports from
operators already in the field. During the 2026 Great ShakeOut exercise,
amateur radio operators across the country and around the globe came
together, joining forces as a dedicated community to demonstrate why
auxiliary communications remain a vital lifeline for disaster response and
seismic science.

The 2026 exercise was designed and managed end-to-end by ARES LAX Northeast
and the Emcomm Training Organization (ETO), led by Oliver Dully, K6OLI, and
Bob Tykulsker, KM6SO, in direct operational alignment with the U.S.
Geological Survey (USGS). Major elements included:

Scientific Collaboration: The USGS earthquake hazards team continues to
pioneer institutional openness to auxiliary radio data, establishing a
real-world testing ground for zero-infrastructure citizen science.

Exercise Architecture: ARES LAX Northeast and ETO developed the scenarios,
standardized templates, GIS ingestion pipelines, and automated reporting
dashboards.

Field Advocacy & Global Reach: Turnout spanning 43 states and 11 nations was
driven by countless local and regional champions, including European and
Caribbean partners, specialized training groups like Winlink Treff and
WLNET-OE, RATPAC, regional nets across the US, and many emergency
coordinators who mobilized their local sections and clubs. A big thank you
to all who advocated for ShakeOut and made this a meaningful local exercise.

The response was swift, disciplined, and massive. As the operational window
headed into its final weekend, the USGS event portal had received over 670
direct "Did You Feel It?" (DYFI) reports originated by amateur radio
operators. More than 610 individual participants mobilized across 43 US
states and 11 countries, routing well over 1,100 digital DYFI, Preparedness
Survey, and Quiz messages via Winlink Express Telnet, Packet, PACTOR, and
VARA protocols.

Scientific Utility Meets Tactical Rigor

Unlike routine check-in nets, the 2026 ShakeOut demanded end-to-end data
precision. Using standardized Winlink templates, hams compiled simulated
seismic intensity reports-quantifying shaking levels, damage observations,
and building performance-and transmitted them directly into the USGS
scientific pipeline.

Beyond raw volume, the exercise provided valuable data-hygiene insights,
highlighting key areas like geocoding consistency between street addresses
and coordinate pairs that will inform future template refinements and
operator training.

This joint drill validated that amateur radio is not merely a backup voice
channel; it is an agile, zero-infrastructure data pipeline capable of
feeding mission-critical ground truth into national emergency dashboards
when commercial internet and cellular systems go dark.

In addition to Winlink reporting, local voice and data nets managed
real-time check-ins and check-outs across participating regions. For
example, the regional SoCal ShakeOut net, co-hosted by San Diego ARES and
ARES LAX, logged 43 active participants, while numerous other local groups
operated dedicated ShakeOut nets to handle regional traffic and coordinate
ground team reports.

Proven Knowledge and Deep Preparedness

Alongside seismic reports, participants tackled an earthquake science and
preparedness quiz as part of an extensive all-hazards preparedness audit:

Earthquake Science & Preparedness Knowledge: Operators posted an exceptional
94% average score on the exercise quiz, demonstrating mastery of earthquake
science fundamentals, USGS intensity terminology (MMI vs. magnitude), and
recommended protective actions like Drop, Cover, and Hold On.

Station & Household Readiness: On a 25-point comprehensive preparedness
scale measuring essential supplies, emergency power, communications gear,
and household plans, participants averaged 18.9 out of 25 (75.6%).

These figures challenge the misconception that disaster exercises are merely
hobbyist drills. They prove that participating amateur radio operators are
well-equipped, knowledgeable, and genuinely invested in community resilience.

Looking Ahead

With the operational submission window now closed, final figures and
post-exercise maps will be consolidated and shared across regional teams.
The data gathered from the ShakeOut scenario demonstrates that effective
disaster response begins at home and extends into our communities. Moving
forward, operators and community members alike are urged to strengthen
personal, family, and neighborhood preparedness-ensuring household safety
and local resilience while continuing to serve as a vital ground-truth
reporting network for the USGS during major seismic events.

Detailed map dashboards, participation breakdowns, and exercise resources
remain available at https://www.emcomm-training.org/Winlink_Thursdays.html
and laxnortheast.org/exercises/shakeout. - Oliver Dully, K6OLI (DEC, ARES
LAX Northeast), and Bob Tykulsker, KM6SO (ETO)


Amateur Radio at the Elm Creek (Minnesota) Half Marathon
--------------------------------------------------------
September 12, 2026--With just over 1100 runners, this was our second year
supporting the event in Elm Creek Park Reserve, just northwest of
Minneapolis. At 4,900 acres, this large park has several hills that can
block repeater signals. The race director was out of town for this one, so
extra time was allocated to brief hams on the route safety details. We were
provided (and were part of) a detailed medical plan. Half marathons can have
serious health situations, particularly with heatstroke, so we had the full
list of capabilities-a medical tent, EMS rigs, aid stations, and running
medics, who have a red shirt and first aid fanny pack to provide care in a
hurry.

Hams were assigned to complex trail crossings. At my stop, we again saw the
ability of runners, presumably "in the zone" or wearing earbuds, to blast
through signs, arrows, and cones down the wrong trail at intersections. This
kept us entertained (we learned the proper wave) and we kept an eye out for
injuries.

The medical director was not a ham, and we had to keep her and her team, and
the other race officials informed. We ended up handing them a handheld on
"scan" mode so they could listen in. We are now testing PTT-over-cellular
(POC) radios at a larger event. Best practice at the moment here is probably
Zello at Work.

We signed out a Hennepin County Sheriff amateur service repeater (146.82
MHz) which was not far away and on a massive tower. (My tow vehicle for our
portable repeater trailer was in the repair shop.) This worked well except
at my location, where I could hear the repeater output but could not get in.
Cell coverage was excellent and we had a text chat group.

The runners had to turn right off the trail, do a U-turn to avoid some old
boards, and then go through the water stop and up another hill to the west.
Signs, a cowbell, a dozen orange cones and vigorous waving usually worked.
We had seven operators, and provided an ICS 205 form, used the existing
medical plan, and did not have a net control. Jen Zielinski, KFMPK, a
former EMT, was our lead. With cool weather, we had just one serious injury.
911 was called and a medical cart responded. -Erik Westgard, NY9D, Assistant
Section Emergency Coordinator for Events, ARRL Minnesota Section


Letters: Alaska Earthquake
--------------------------
I enjoyed the September ARES Letter summary of amateur radio responses to
significant events over the years. The 1964 Alaska earthquake, also known as
the Great Alaska Earthquake and the Good Friday Earthquake, occurred at 5:36
PM AKST on Good Friday, March 27, 1964. One of the lower 48 states'
responding stations and maybe the first to receive messages from Alaska, was
Al Francisco, K7NHV. Al was an active member of the ARRL National Traffic
System in Montana at the time and, on that evening, started receiving
emergency traffic from Alaska. He spent the following 2 to 3 days handling
emergency traffic and routing it into the NTS. Many other amateurs were
involved, but Al was only 17 years old at the time. Quite an effort and an
indication of future excellence for Al. -Ward Silver, NAX, St. Charles,
Missouri


Letters: Portable Winlink Sysops Station
----------------------------------------
I was inspired to build a portable Winlink sysops station that I could use
almost anywhere and link to my mini Starlink antenna for the internet. I
live in Alaska and there are not that many options for hitting a Winlink
Sysops base station. I actually live between two of them but due to terrain
and obstacles, I could not hit either of them from my home base, so I
established my own VHF packet base station, but also came up with the idea
of building a portable system. I built a portable unit and the Matanuska
Amateur Radio Association/Club in Wasilla, Alaska, tested it out for the
first time with a mini-Starlink satellite dish, and it worked perfectly.

Now, I am wondering how many other emergency preparedness organizations or
individuals have thought about doing this. I never heard nor read any
discussion or articles about it. Obviously not everyone could do it but it
could be done at other radio clubs and organizations if they were lacking
connectivity in their area. I think about all the communities that had
disasters and normal communications completely wiped out. I can set up a VHF
sysops station and Starlink with an antenna mounted on top of my car or on a
tripod for higher coverage.

I posted on Facebook under Amateur Radio Alaska: "A marriage between Winlink
RMS Express and Starlink Satellite." -Ken Hudson, KL2HF, with thanks to Rich
Moseson, W2VU, The ARRL Letter


FEMA Study Findings
-------------------
ARRL has a formal memorandum of understanding (MOU) with the Federal
Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). The agency and its partners released a
new study that provides key insights into challenges faced by emergency
managers across the nation. The "Emergency Management Organizational
Structures, Staffing, and Capacity Study" confirms that the country's
emergency management system is strongest when it is locally led, state
managed, and federally supported. The study surveyed over 1,700 emergency
management officials nationwide and found that local and state agencies are
best positioned to understand and address the unique needs of their
communities.

"FEMA is focused on empowering state, local, tribal, and territorial
governments to lead through all phases of emergency management," said
Administrator Cameron Hamilton. "This study makes clear that a
one-size-fits-all, federally driven approach will never be as effective as
community-led efforts. FEMA will continue to partner with officials at every
level of government, empowering them to build local capabilities and invest
in resilience efforts."

FEMA sponsored the study to better understand the emergency management
landscape. The report finds that local emergency management agencies are
critical to our nation's resilience, but it also finds that those
organizations are typically understaffed and underfunded.

For decades, many states have relied excessively on federal grants to
maintain emergency response capabilities-to their own detriment. To
strengthen their resilience against disasters and emergencies, states,
tribal nations, territories, and local jurisdictions should consistently
invest in their own emergency management capabilities.

This study was funded by FEMA and conducted by the Argonne National
Laboratory, Big City Emergency Managers (BCEM), the International
Association of Emergency Managers (IAEM-USA), and the National Emergency
Management Association (NEMA), which also collaborated on the study.

In addition to publishing the study's findings as a report, FEMA worked with
the Argonne National Laboratory to launch the National Emergency Management
Data Explorer (NEMDEX) tool. This tool is available to help review and
analyze the summary information gathered from the local survey component of
the study. Community leaders can review the findings to make informed,
strategic decisions on investment and capacity building efforts.

To view the report and access the NEMDEX tool, please visit the Argonne
National Laboratory.

The Amateur Radio Emergency Service© (ARES) consists of licensed amateurs
who have voluntarily registered their qualifications and equipment with
their local ARES leadership, for communications duty in the public service
when disaster strikes. Every licensed amateur, regardless of membership in
ARRL or any other local or national organization is eligible to apply for
membership in ARES. Training may be required or desired to participate fully
in ARES. Please inquire at the local level for specific information. Because
ARES is an amateur radio program, only licensed radio amateurs are eligible
for membership. The possession of emergency-powered equipment is desirable,
but is not a requirement for membership.

How to Get Involved in ARES: Fill out the ARES Registration form and submit
it to your local Emergency Coordinator.
